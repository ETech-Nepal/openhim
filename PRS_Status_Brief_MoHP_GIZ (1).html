02 OpenHIM Initial Design & Channel Planning Done — 3 channels live

This is our proposed design. The channel set, the routing sequence and the role boundaries were designed and built by TechBucket and are put forward here for MoHP review. Three channels are set up and running in the console: NP FHIR Orchestrator, NP Patient Admin Review and NP Patient Portal — each with its own access role.

NP FHIR Orchestrator the main clinical route

  • Validate the incoming bundle against Nepal's national FHIR profiles (np.core) — so bad data is rejected at the door rather than after it has been saved.
  • Look the patient up in the Master Patient Index to determine whether this person already exists nationally.
  • Attach the national Health ID, replacing the hospital's own record number. This is the step that lets records from different hospitals be linked to one person.

NP Patient Admin Review the human check

  • Where the system is not certain, a person decides. Our proposal is that MoHP data stewards confirm a match, reject it, merge two golden records, or unmerge one that was joined in error.
  • Every decision is attributable and reversible — merges can be undone.

NP Patient Portal the citizen route

  • The route by which a patient reaches their own record using their Health ID, scoped so a portal credential can only ever read that one person's data.
  • Detailed scope to be discussed and agreed with MoHP in a later session.

We would welcome MoHP’s review of this channel design — particularly the role boundaries and whether any further channel is required before the remaining hospitals are onboarded.
03 Health ID Algorithm & Matching Rules Design Under discussion — proposal on the table

The algorithm is under discussion with the MoHP IT team — the question of which check digit algorithm to adopt is open, and the decision rests with MoHP. What we have done is build and test the options in Go so the discussion rests on measured behaviour rather than preference. Our recommendation is Damm. A working implementation is running in the deployed environment so the choice can be seen operating before it is settled.

  • Four algorithms implemented and tested — Damm, Luhn, Verhoeff and ISBN-10. Each was built and run against realistic error patterns rather than assessed on paper.
  • Implemented and tested in Go. The generation and check digit logic is written as a standalone Go service with a test suite covering single-digit substitution, adjacent transposition and the letter-to-digit mapping. Testing in code rather than on paper is what let us compare the four algorithms on measured behaviour, and the same service is what runs in the deployed environment today.
  • We recommend Damm. It is the only one of the four that catches every adjacent-digit transposition — the exact error made when an ID is read aloud or hand-keyed at a counter. Verhoeff performs comparably but is materially harder to implement correctly; Luhn misses transpositions; ISBN-10 introduces a non-numeric check character.
  • The check digit still covers the letters. Damm is a mod-10 scheme and cannot read letters directly, so each letter is mapped to two digits before the check digit is computed — A to 00, B to 01, through to Z at 25. Damm then runs over all ten digits, meaning a mistyped letter is caught exactly like a mistyped number. This avoids needing an alphanumeric scheme such as ISO/IEC 7064 MOD 37,36, which is materially harder to implement correctly.
  • Proposed format — a two-letter series, six random digits and one check digit. Nine characters in total, running from AA000000‑x to ZZ999999‑x: short enough to read aloud over a phone, write on a card and key by hand without error.
  • The ID carries no embedded personal, geographic or facility data — a design principle we have proposed, so the number can be printed, quoted and shared without disclosing anything about the person. This holds whichever check digit algorithm is adopted.
  • Capacity — 676 million. Six digits alone give one million, which is below Nepal's population; the AA–ZZ series multiplies that by 676. Series AA is issued first and the next is opened only as the previous nears exhaustion, so the series carries no meaning and reveals nothing about the holder.
  • Lifecycle rules drafted for reissue, deactivation, merge and unmerge — under review with the MoHP IT team.

Excluding the letters I and O, which are misread as 1 and 0 when handwritten or read aloud, reduces the series to 24 × 24 = 576 and the total to 576 million — still 19× headroom, while removing an error class the check digit would otherwise have to absorb. Cheaper to decide now than after the first million identifiers are issued.

Matching rules

  • Matching runs mostly on NHID / Health ID, so in practice this is not expected to be a problem — the identifier is present in the large majority of cases and resolves the patient outright.
  • A weighted method is proposed for cases where no identifier is presented, scoring on mobile number, date of birth and name. We suggest this as the fallback rather than the default; the comparators and thresholds are still to be settled.
  • Existing identifiers are kept, not replaced — Health ID, national ID, insurance number and the hospital's own record number all sit on the same patient, so no existing system loses its own reference. Proposed and not contested.

Issuance rule — proposed for MoHP decision

The Health ID stands on its own. It is a random number with a check digit, computed from nothing, so it does not depend on the national ID being present, correct, or available. What identifiers a person carries determines how confidently we matched them, not whether they receive a Health ID.

NID presentHealth ID issued · deterministic match on NID · assurance high
No NID, BIMA presentHealth ID issued · deterministic match on BIMA · assurance high
NeitherHealth ID issued · weighted match on mobile, date of birth, name · assurance moderate, referred to stewardship where ambiguous

We propose this rule deliberately: if an NID were required for issuance, the Health ID would cease to be an independent health identifier and would exclude the populations least likely to hold one — minors, remote communities, the displaced. Same identifier for everyone; assurance level recorded alongside it.
04 OpenHIM Production Configuration In progress — active now

Work on this is ongoing and running in parallel with the mediator development. The environment is functional and carrying live traffic; what remains is the hardening required before it can be called production-ready.

  • Working now — channels deploy from code and rebuild identically; confirmed by tearing the environment down and rebuilding it in full.
  • Working now — every request carries a signed token, verified and mapped to the hospital that issued it. Each hospital holds its own key; no shared passwords anywhere.
  • Working now — three channels enabled and carrying traffic from two hospitals independently, each on its own credential.
  • In hand — encrypted mutual connections between the hospital mediators and the layer; secrets management and network segmentation.
  • Still to do — alerting and escalation rules; backup, restore and the disaster recovery runbook including failover to Hetauda; and performance validation against the agreed non-functional targets.

The non-functional targets need confirming before performance validation can be run against them. We would propose settling these with MoHP in the same session as the terminology scoping.
05 Health ID System Implementation (JeMPI) Done
  • JeMPI deployed and integrated behind the exchange layer. The ToR scoped OpenCR/OpenEMPI; after evaluation we suggested JeMPI to the MoHP IT team and the change was agreed in joint session before implementation began.
  • Registration works end to end: the system decides whether the person already exists, issues a new Health ID or returns the existing one, and stores a single record.
  • Cross-facility duplicate detection proven live. The same patient submitted from Bharatpur Hospital and then from Bakulahar Ratnanagar Hospital, on two separate credentials, came back with the same Health ID and the same underlying record — no duplicate created.
  • A hospital cannot impersonate another. A submission claiming a facility other than the one it authenticated as is rejected.
  • Matching cost is paid once. Resolution happens at registration; once the Health ID is known, later lookups go straight to the record store and never trouble the matching engine.
  • Remaining — the Health ID API through OpenHIM (match, link, unlink, cross-reference), the stewardship console for MoHP staff, and accuracy figures once the HIB cohort is available.
  • Proposed next step — seed from HIB. Rather than waiting for patients to present, we propose HIB transfer their enrolled population into the layer so Health IDs are generated in advance. Because HIB enrolment records already carry the NID, this delivers both identifiers in a single health-sector transfer.
  • This removes NID integration from the critical path. The NID is recorded as present-unverified and matching runs on it immediately — an unverified NID is still a sound matching key. Direct NID integration later simply flips the status to verified, with no reprocessing and no re-issuance.

Sequenced as fill rate → sample extract → threshold calibration → full load. Calibrating on a sample first matters: a bulk load into an untuned matcher creates duplicates that then have to be unpicked by hand, which is far more expensive at population scale than tuning thresholds on a few thousand records.
06 Terminology Service (HAPI FHIR + OCL) Not started — scheduled

Substantive work on this has not yet begun. Effort to date has gone into the interoperability layer, the Health ID and the hospital mediators — the workstreams that had to be proven first. Terminology is scheduled next and the full ten days of allocated effort remain available.

  • Dependency — the terminology content cannot be settled before the base FHIR implementation guide is confirmed, since the guide determines which value sets are bound and enforced. That confirmation is one of our asks below.
  • To do — stand up Open Concept Lab as the environment where MoHP authors and curates codes, and configure the terminology runtime that serves them to hospitals.
  • To do — structure and load the observation and clinical code sets exercised by the pilot patient flow, bound to international standards where they exist and to national code systems where they do not.
  • To do — expose lookup, validate, expand and translate through the terminology channel, and author the mapping tables the pilot hospitals need.
  • Later phase — full content load including ICD-11 with ICD-10 mapping so HMIS reporting continues to work, plus medicines, laboratory and imaging catalogues and administrative geography, and mapping onto DHIS2 data elements.

This is the largest single body of work remaining and the one most dependent on MoHP input, since the code sets are a national content decision rather than an engineering task. We would propose starting the joint sessions on terminology in parallel with the IG confirmation rather than waiting for it.
07 EMR Mediator Development Tested — main focus of effort

This is where most of our time goes — the hospital side. Two pilot hospitals are connected and tested, exchanging data successfully.

  • The whole patient journey tested end to end at both hospitals — registration, encounter, visit, observation and clinical documentation in a single validated pathway.
  • Each hospital authenticates independently with its own credential.
  • Onboarding is now packaged, not bespoke. Integration scripts and a vendor starter kit mean each further hospital is configuration and testing rather than fresh development — this is what makes national rollout affordable.
  • Sandbox with synthetic patient data available for vendors to build against without touching real records.
  • Remaining — onboard the remaining pilot hospitals, add the referral and back-referral leg, and capture formal test evidence for acceptance.

Going forward

2 · Connect with the HIB IT department — 40,000–50,000 records carrying NID

Open question for MoHP — HIB or NID as the seeding source?

The two routes lead to the same place but differ in what they cost and what they prove. We would put the choice to MoHP rather than assume it.

Via HIBRecords already carry both NID and BIMA. A health-sector transfer, so no external data-sharing instrument is needed and it can start quickly. The NID is recorded as present-but-unverified — sound for matching, but not proof the number is genuine. Covers the enrolled population only.
Via NIDAuthoritative at source and gives national coverage rather than the insured population. But it is a civil registry outside the health sector, so it needs a formal data-sharing agreement and a longer approval path than the contract period comfortably allows.
Our suggestionStart with HIB, keep the NID conversation open in parallel. HIB gives a calibration cohort now; NID integration later upgrades assurance from unverified to verified without reprocessing or re-issuing anything. The two are sequential, not alternatives.
